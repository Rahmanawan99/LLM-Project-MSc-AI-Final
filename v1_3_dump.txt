[76] (Heading 1) ABSTRACT
[77] (Normal) This project investigates the problem of factual hallucinations in small open-source language models. Where models generate responses that may be plausible but factually incorrect or unsupported. The research evaluated whether Retrieval-Augmented Generation RAG, combined with Semantic Consistency Evaluation (SCE), can improve the reliability of the text generated by such models. A controlled framework and a series of investigations, samples and runs compare scores all the way from baseline model to RAG enhanced and a RAG enhanced with SCE.
[78] (Normal) Using Flan-T5-Small (77 million parameters) and the TruthfulQA benchmark dataset (817 adversarial questions), a controlled experimental framework compared three conditions: a baseline language model, the same model enhanced with RAG, and a RAG-based system incorporating SCE.
[79] (Normal) The results demonstrated that RAG significantly improved factual accuracy, with ROUGE-L scores increasing from 0.088 to 0.357 (a 306% improvement, p < 10⁻⁷⁶, Cohen's d = 0.81) and semantic similarity to ground-truth answers rising from 0.256 to 0.458. The SCE module, operating as a post-hoc hallucination detector using cosine similarity between generated answers and retrieved context, achieved an F1 score of 0.709 (precision = 0.68, recall = 0.74). Responses identified as semantically consistent scored 3.5 times higher on ROUGE-L than those flagged as potential hallucinations. Category-level analysis across 38 question types revealed hallucination rates ranging from 0% (Finance) to 89% (Subjective), with two categories showing degraded performance under RAG due to retrieval of misleading context.
[80] (Normal) The findings confirm that retrieval augmentation combined with lightweight semantic consistency checking provides a practical and computationally efficient method for improving the factual reliability and trustworthiness of small language models, while also identifying the conditions under which these approaches succeed and fail.
[81] (Normal) Keywords: Large Language Models, Hallucinations, Retrieval-Augmented Generation, Semantic Consistency Evaluation, Open-Source, Small Language Models, TruthfulQA, Factual Accuracy, ROUGE, Cosine Similarity

[96] (Heading 1) Chapter 1: Introduction
[97] (Heading 2) 1.1 Background
[98] (Normal) Large Language models have achieved remarkable progress in recent years, demonstrating capabilities in text generation, question answering, summarization, and reasoning that were once considered beyond the reach of automated systems. Models such as GPT-4, PaLM, and LLaMA have pushed boundaries of what natural language processing can accomplish with applications spanning healthcare, education, legal analysis and scientific research (Huang et al., 2025). However, these advances come with a significant and well-documented limitation: the tendency of language models to generate information that appears fluent and plausible but is factually incorrect, unsorted or entirely fabricated. This phenomenon, widely referred to as hallucination, poses a serios barrier to the reliable deployment of language models in contexts where factual accuracy matters (Ji et al, 2023).
[99] (Normal) The problem of hallucination is not confined to any single model family or architecture. Both encoder-decoder models and autoregressive transformers exhibit these behaviours, and it persists across model scales ranging from compact models with fewer than 100 million parameters to the largest proprietary systems (Huang et al.,2025). What makes hallucination articulacy challenging is that the generated text often maintains grammatical correctness and surface level coherence, making it difficult for end users to distinguish between factual and fabricated content without external verification. In domains such as medical advice, legal guidance, and educational content, such errors carry tangible risks. Retrieval-Augmented generation, or RAG, has emerged as one of the most widely adopted strategies for mitigating hallucinations.
[100] (Normal) Originally proposed by Lewis et al. (2020), RAG combines a parametric language model with a non-parametric retrieval component that fetches relevant documents from an external knowledge base at inference time.  The retrieved passages are then incorporated into the generation process, effectible grounding the model’s output in evidence rather than relying solely on learned parameters.
[101] (Normal) Several studies have demonstrated that RAG can substantially improve factual accuracy across a range of knowledge-intensive tasks (Shuster et al.,2021; Geo et al., 2023). Nevertheless, RAG alone does not fully resolve the hallucination problem. Even when relevant context is successfully retrieved, language models may ignore, misinterpret, or contradict the retrieved information during generation (Asai et al., 2024). This has motivated research into additional verification mechanisms that can assess whether a generated response is supported by the retrieved evidence. Semantic consistent evaluations represent one such approach, using dense vector representations to measure the alignment between a generated answer and its supporting context.
[102] (Normal) 
[103] (Heading 2) 1.2 Problem Statement
[104] (Normal) While large proprietary models such as GPT-4 and Claude benefit from massive training corpora, reinforcement learning from human feedback and internal safer mechanisms, small open-source language models typically lack these advantages. Models in the range of 80/250 million parameters, such as Flan-T5-Small, are attractive for research and deployment in resource constrained environments due to their low computational requirements and accessibility. However, these smaller models are particularly susceptible to hallucination because their limited parameter count restricts the volume of factual knowledge; they can reality store and recall (Chung et al. 2024; Gunasekara et al. 2023).
[105] (Normal) The central problem this research addresses are whether a combination of RAG and post generation semantic consistency checking can meaningfully reduce hallucination rates in such small models. While RAG has been studied extensively for larger models, its effectiveness when paired with compact architecture remains less well understood. Similarly, semantic consistency evaluation as a standalone hallucination detection mechanism has received limited empirical investigation in the context of small-scale systems.

[123] (Heading 2) 1.5 Significance
[124] (Normal) This research contributes to the growing body of work on making language models more trustworthy without requiring the computational resources associated with frontier models. If small models can be made substantially more reliable through retrieval augmentation and lightweight semantic checking, this has practical implications for deployment in educational tools, low-resource applications, and settings where cloud-based access to large proprietary models is not feasible. The study also provides empirical evidence on the effectiveness of cosine-similarity-based consistency checking as a detection mechanism, an approach that has seen limited formal evaluation in literature.

[128] (Heading 2) 1.6 Scope
[129] (Normal) This research evaluates three small open-source language models operating below the 1-billion parameter threshold: Flan-T5-Small (77M parameters, encoder-decoder), Flan-T5-Base (250M parameters, encoder-decoder) and Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct (500M parameters, decoder-only). [131] (Heading 1) Chapter 2: Literature Review
[132] (Heading 2) 2.1 Overview of Hallucination in Language Models
[133] (Normal) This literature review synthesizes two recent foundational works, Ji et al. (2023), Huang et al. (2025) to provide a comprehensive overview of the detection and mitigation of hallucinations in Large Language Models (LLMs), specifically through the lens of Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and semantic consistency.
[134] (Normal) Both sources define hallucination as the generation of content that is fluent and coherent yet, factually incorrect, logically inconsistent or ungrounded Liu et al. (2023).
[135] (Normal) Huang et al. (2025) categorise hallucinations into factuality hallucinations (conflicts with real world facts) and faithfulness hallucinations (diverging from user context). This aligns with Anh-Hoang et al. (2025) broader classification of intrinsic versus extrinsic hallucinations.
[136] (Normal) The sources identify several methodologies for detention with a heavy emphasis on eternal uncertainty and consistency. Huag et al. (2025) highlights uncertainty estimation as primary detection strategy categories into methods analysing LLMS internal states and LLM behaviour 12
[137] (Normal) Anh-Hoang et al. (2025) propose the Joint Attribution Score (JAS) to quantify interactive effects between prompts and models serving as a diagnostic tool to identify when a specific prompt-model combination is likely to amplify errors.
[138] (Normal) Both papers recognize Retrieval-Augments Generation (RAG) as a potent strategy to supply LLMs with non-parametric, up-to-date knowledge. Despite its benefits Huang et al. (2025) identify retrieval failure and generation bottlenecks as primary cause of hallucination systems.
[139] (Normal) As per Bergeron et al. (2025), we can also see that small language models: SlMS can achieve competition results with frontier models when they are optimized for reasoning rather than completions.
[140] (Normal) The HalluGuard-4B model HalluGuard (Bergeron et al., 2025) demonstrates that a 4B-Paratemerer backbone can rival 30B+ parameter models like Llama 3.1 70B in hallucination detections when fine-tuned using preference alignment.
[141] (Normal) The sources collectively argue that for small open-source models, retrieval and consistency checks are not just additive by complementarity. RAG extends the model’s factual reach, while semantic consistency provides the meta-cognitive check necessary to ensure that the retrieved information is used correctly and that internal knowledge gaps are identified.
[142] (Normal) This combined approach allows models as small as 4B parameters to exhibit factual reliability previously only associated with much larger closed source systems.
[143] (Normal) Even though our project is not scaled for multiple or cross language cases, we have also studied papers that imply consistency in cross-language frameworks. For this we have seen Rowen (Ding et al., 2024) as a sensitive signal to detect model uncertainty and potential hallucinations before generating a final response.
[144] (Normal) The sources identify several critical gaps in the current research on large language models has, ranging from the inability to distinguish between error sources to limitations in RAG and explainability.
[145] (Heading 2) 2.2 Gap Analysis
[146] (Normal) A primary gap identified is the lack of a formal framework to determine whether a hallucination stems from suboptimal prompting or the model’s intrinsic architecture and training data. We also see current detection methods struggle with models that are "consistently wrong" or overconfident in their fabrications, most importantly the "Consistent Error" Problem: Consistency-based detection often fails when an LLM provides a response that is semantically coherent across multiple perturbations but is factually incorrect.
[147] (Normal) Despite the substantial body of work reviewed above, several gaps remain in the literature that this study aims to address.
[148] (Normal) First, most of the RAG research has focused on larger models with billions of parameters. The effectiveness of RAG when applied to genuinely small models (under 100 million parameters) has received comparatively little attention. It is not self-evident that retrieval augmentation benefits smaller models to the same degree, as their limited generation capacity may constrain their ability to effectively utilize retrieved context.
[149] (Normal) Second, while semantic consistency has been explored through sophisticated methods such as semantic entropy Farquhar et al., (2024) and multi-sample self-checking Manakul, Liusie and Gales, (2023), simpler approaches based on direct cosine similarity between context and response have not been formally evaluated as standalone hallucination detectors. This study examines whether such a lightweight method provides a practically useful signal.
[150] (Normal) Third, there is limited category-level analysis in existing literature. Most studies report aggregate metrics across entire benchmarks, obscuring the significant performance variation that may exist across different question types or knowledge domains.
[151] (Normal) Finally, the interaction between retrieval quality and semantic consistency scoring has not been systematically examined. When retrieval fails, semantic consistency may produce misleading signals, flagging correct answers as inconsistent because the retrieved context is irrelevant or approving incorrect answers because they happen to match irrelevant retrieved passages.
[152] (Normal) The development of capable small language models has accelerated in recent years derived by both academic research and practical demand for deployable systems. Chung et al. (2024) describes the Flan-T5 family, which applies fine-tuning instructions across more than 1,800 tasks to the T5 architecture Raffel et al., (2020). Their results show that instruction tuning can substantially improve zero-shot performance even for the smallest model variants, making Flan-T5-Small a viable choice for research on small models. Gunasekar et al. (2023) demonstrate with the Phi series of models that training data quality can partially compensate for model size. Their 1.3-billion-parameter model, trained predominantly on synthetic textbook-quality data, achieves reasoning performance competitive with models an order of magnitude larger. This finding is significant because it suggests that the limitations of small models are not solely attributable to parameter count but are also influenced by training data composition and distribution.

[157] (Heading 2) 3.1 Research Design
[158] (Normal) This study adopts a quantitive research design, structured around a controlled comparison of three experimental conditions applied to the same benchmark dataset.
[159] (List Paragraph) Condition A: Baseline LLM: The language model generates answers to questions directly, without access to any external knowledge source.
[160] (List Paragraph) Condition B: RAG: This model generates answers with support of contact aware retrieval from an external knowledge base via dense vector similarity search
[161] (List Paragraph) Condition C RAG + SCE: The RAG generated answers are subsequently evaluated by a semantic consistency module that computes the cosine similarity between each generated answer and its retrieved context, flagging responses to a defined threshold as potential hallucinations.
[162] (Normal) 
[163] (Normal) This three-condition design enables a progressive evaluation of each component's contribution: RAG's impact on factual accuracy can be isolated by comparing Conditions A and B, while the SCE module's detection capability can be assessed through the quality differential between flagged and unflagged responses in Condition C.

[172] (Heading 2) 3.3 Data Preprocessing
[173] (Normal) Minimal pre-processing was applied to the TruthfulQA dataset, as the questions and reference answers are already in clean text format. The only pre-processing was the construction of knowledgebase for the RAG pipeline. For each 817 questions, the corresponding best_answer was extracted and stored as a text document in the retrieval corpus. Each was then encoded into a dimensional vectorizing the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 sentence transformer model (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019). It should be noted that the use of best_answer as the knowledge base creates a controlled experimental setting where the retrieval step has access to the ground-truth answers. This design choice allows the study to isolate the generation and consistency evaluation components by ensuring that retrieval quality is not the primary bottleneck. The implications and limitations of this decision are discussed in Chapter 6.

[178] (Heading 2) 3.5 Evaluation Metrics
[179] (Normal) The following metrics were employed to assess performance across the three experimental conditions:
[180] (List Paragraph) ROUGE Scores: ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, and ROUGE-L F1 scores (Lin, 2004) measure n-gram and longest common subsequence overlap between the generated answer and the reference best_answer. These metrics capture lexical similarity but do not account for semantic equivalence.
[181] (List Paragraph) Semantic similarity: The cosine similarity between the sentence embeddings of the generated answer and the reference best_answer, computed using all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019). This metric captures semantic closeness regardless of lexical variation.
[182] (List Paragraph) Semantic Consistent Evaluation Score: The cosine similarity between the sentence embeddings of the generated answer and the retrieved context. This metric, specific to Conditions B and C, quantifies the degree to which the generated response is grounded in the retrieved evidence.
[183] (List Paragraph) Hallucination Rate: The proportion of responses where the SCE score falls below the threshold  = 0.5. Responses below this threshold are flagged as potential hallucinations on the basis that they are semantically dissimilar to their supporting context.
[184] (List Paragraph) SCE Classifier Metrics: Using ROUGE-L < 0.2 as a proxy ground-truth label for actual hallucinations, the SCE flagging mechanism is evaluated as a binary classifier in terms of precision, recall, and F1 score.
[185] (List Paragraph) Statistical Significance: The Wilcoxon signed-rank test (non-parametric, paired) is used to test whether differences between conditions are statistically significant at  = 0.05. Effect sizes are reported using Cohen's d, with values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 interpreted as small, medium, and large effects respectively. Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (5,000 iterations) are reported for key metrics.

[187] (Heading 2) 3.6 Justification of methods used
[188] (Normal) The choice of Flan-T5-Small as the generator model is motivated by the study's focus on small open-source models. With approximately 77 million parameters, it sits at the lower end of practical model sizes while still benefiting from instruction tuning (Chung et al., 2024). The T5 encoder-decoder architecture (Raffel et al., 2020) is well-suited to conditional generation tasks where the input includes both a question and retrieved context.
[189] (Normal) To assess the generalisability of the RAG and SCE framework, two additional language models were evaluated. Flan-T5-Base (Chung et al., 2024), with approximately 250 million parameters, was selected to examine whether the benefits of RAG scale with model size within the same encoder-decoder architecture. Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct (Qwen Team, 2024), a 500-million-parameter decoder-only autoregressive model, was selected to test whether the framework generalises across fundamentally different model architectures. The model selection was informed by hardware compatibility analysis using LLMFit (Jones, 2026), an open-source tool for matching language models to local hardware constraints.
[190] (Normal) Hardware compatibility was assessed using LLMFit (Jones, 2026), an open-source command-line tool that analyses local hardware specifications (GPU VRAM, system RAM, CPU) and recommends language models that can be run within those constraints. This tool confirmed that the Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct model could be loaded and executed within the available 6 GB VRAM of the NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050 GPU.
[191] (Normal) The all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embedding model was selected for its balance between embedding quality and computational efficiency. Producing 384-dimensional vectors, it enables rapid similarity computation while maintaining strong performance on semantic textual similarity benchmarks (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019).
[192] (Normal) The FAISS flat L2 index was chosen over approximate indexing methods because the knowledge base contains only 817 documents, making exact search both feasible and appropriate. For larger corpora, approximate indexing techniques such as HNSW (Malkov and Yashunin, 2020) would be preferred.
[193] (Normal) The hallucination threshold of  = 0.5 was selected as a midpoint and subsequently validated through a sensitivity analysis across thresholds ranging from 0.05 to 0.95.
[194] (Normal) The results of this analysis, presented in Chapter 5, demonstrate that the choice of threshold affects the flagging rate but does not fundamentally alter the relative quality differential between flagged and unflagged responses.

[300] (Heading 2) 5.5 Failure Case Analysis
[301] (Normal) Examination of the 20 worst performing responses reveal several recurring failure patterns:
[302] (List Paragraph) Pattern 1: Degenerate outputs: In some cases, the model produces output such as single words, Roman numerals (e.g., “(iii)”), or repetitive strings (e.g., "John Roosevelt & John Roosevelt & John Roosevelt..."). These failures appear to stem from the models’ limited capacity to handle ambiguous or complex prompts, reverting to degenerate modes.
[303] (List Paragraph) Pattern 2: Retrieval mismatch: When the FAISS index returns a semantically related but factually incorrect document, the model faithfully incorporates the misleading context. For example, a question about Paul Revere’s midnight ride retrieved a passage about Paul McCartney, resulting in a completely unrelated answer.
[304] (List Paragraph) Pattern 3: Over-compression: The model frequently reduces multi word reference answers to single tokens. For the question about what U.S. state produces the most peaches, the model answers “California” rather than “California produces the most peaches in the U.S.” While factually correct, this extreme compression reduces both ROUGE-L and SCE scores because the abbreviated responses have low surface overlap with both the reference and the retrieved context.

[306] (Heading 2) 5.6 Discussion
[307] (Normal) The experimental results provide clear answers to the three research questions posed in Chapter 1.
[308] (Normal) Regarding RQ1, the RAG pipeline improves factual accuracy substantially, with a 306% increase in ROUGE-L and a 79% increase in sematic similarity relative to the baseline. The improvement is statistically significant with large effect sizes, confirming that even a very small language model can meaningfully benefit from external knowledge retrieval. This finding extends the existing literature on RAG, which has predominantly focused on larger models, the sub-100-million parameter regime.
[309] (Normal) Regarding RQ2, the SCE module demonstrates meaningful discriminative ability as a hallucination detector, achieving an F1 score of 0.709. While not a perfect classifier, it provides a useful signal: responses it identifies as consistent score 3.5 times higher on ROUGE-L than those it flags. The bimodal distribution of SCE scores suggests that the boundary between grounded and ungrounded responses is reasonably well defined for this model and dataset combination.
[310] (Normal) Regarding RQ3, the category level analysis reveals substantial variation in performance. Categories involving straightforward factual recall benefit most from RAG, while categories requiring reasoning, disambiguation, or subjective judgement remain challenging. The identification of two categories where RAG worsens performance highlights an important limitation: retrieval is not universally beneficial, and the quality of retrieved documents is a critical variable.

[333] (Heading 1) Chapter 6: Conclusion
[334] (Heading 2) 6.1 Summary of key findings
[335] (Normal) This study investigated whether Retrieved-Augmented Generation combined with Semantic Consistency Evaluation can reduce factual hallucinations in small open-source language models. Using Flan-T5-Small (77 million parameters), Flan-T5-Base (250M parameters), and Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct (500M parameters), and the TruthfulQA benchmark (817 adversarial questions), a three-condition experimental framework was implemented and evaluated.
[336] (Normal) The principal findings are as follows, First, RAG significantly improves the factual accuracy of small language models. The ROUGLE-L score increased from 0.088 t0 0.357, a 306% improvement that is statistically significant (p < 10^-76) with a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.81). Second, the SCE module provides a practical hallucination detection mechanism, achieving an F1 score of 0.709 without requiring any supervised training or model fine tuning. Responses identified as semantically consistent with their retrieved context scored 3.5 times higher on ROUGE-L than flagged responses. Third, performance varies substantially across question categories, with hallucination rates ranging from 0% in Finance to 89% in Subjective and two categories showing degraded performance under RAG due to retrieved of misleading context.
[337] (Heading 2) 6.2 Research contributions
[338] (Normal) This research makes three contributions to the field. It provides empirical evidence that RAG is effective for genuinely small language models (under 100 million parameters), extending findings previously established only for larger architectures. It evaluates cosine-similarity-based semantic consistency as a lightweight hallucination detection mechanism, demonstrating that this simple approach produces a practically useful discriminative signal. It offers a detailed category-level analysis that reveals the conditions under which RAG succeeds and fails, providing guidance for practitioners deploying retrieval-augmented systems.
[339] (Heading 2) 6.3 Limitations
[340] (Normal) Several limitations should be acknowledged. The knowledge base used in the RAG pipeline was construction from the TruthfulQA ground truth answers rather than an independent external corpus. While this design choice was deliberate to isolate the generation and evaluation components it means the retrieval step has access to the correct answers which would not be the case in a real-world deployment. The results therefore represent an upper bound on RAGs effectiveness under ideal retrieval conditions.
[341] (Normal) The SCE threshold of =0.5, while validated through sensitivity analysis, remains a design parameter without a principled theoretical justification. The optimal threshold may vary across domains, datasets, and model architecture.
[342] (Normal) While the study evaluates three language models across two architectural paradigms (encoder-decoder and decoder-only), all evaluated systems remain below the 1-billion parameter threshold. Generalisation to larger foundation models (e.g., 7B to 70B parameters) and closed-source proprietary APIs remains an area for broader empirical validation. The evaluation relies entirely on automated metrics; human evaluation of answer quality was not conducted. Finally, the BERTScore (Zhan et al., 2020) was originally planned as an additional evaluation metric but could not be computed due to a version compatibility issue between the BERTScore library and the installed transformers package. Including BERTScore would have provided a token level semantic evaluation complementing the sentence level and n-gram level metrics used.

[345] (Heading 1) Chapter 7: Future Work
[346] (Heading 2) Summary of future work
[347] (Normal) Several directions for extending this research merit consideration. Some of which are highlighted in this section.
[348] (Normal) Firstly, we will replace the TruthfulQA knowledge base with a genuine external corpus such as Wikipedia or web search results. This would test the pipeline under realistic, retrieved conditions where the correct answer may not be present in the corpus and where the retrieved documents may contain noise or partial information.
[349] (Normal) More forms of future work include the most common; evaluating scores across multiple model scales, in our project comparing scores across Flan-T5-Small (77M), Flan-T5-Base (250M), and Flan-T5-Large (780M) would establish whether the benefits of RAG and SCE scale with model capacity or diminish as the model’s parametric knowledge improves.
[350] (Normal) The cross-model comparison revealed that the fixed SCE threshold of t=0.5 is effective for Faln-T5 models but produces a high false-positive rate for Qwen2.5-0.5B, which generates far fewer hallucinations. Future work could explore adaptive thresholds calibrated per model using a validation set or learned threshold using supervised classification.
[351] (Normal) Like multimodal score evaluation we can also perform a multi semantic consistency comparison, the current SCE module competes a single generated answer against the retrieved context. A more robust approach would generate multiple responses to the same prompt (using temperature sampling or nucleus sampling) and measure the semantic consistency across these responses, following the approach of Manakul, Liusie and Gales (2023). Responses that are inconsistent across samples are more likely to be hallucinated, providing an additional detection signal.
[352] (Normal) And finally incorporating natural language inference and human evaluation could be extended to the score. replacing or supplementing cosine similarity with natural language inference (NLI) classifiers would enable the SCE module to assess whether the generated answer is entailed by the retrieved context, rather than merely semantically similar. With natural language inference, conducting a human evaluation study where annotators assess the factual accuracy, relevance, and fluency of generated responses across conditions would provide validation of the automated metrics and reveal quality dimensions not captured by ROUGE or cosine similarity.
